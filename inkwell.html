Case study · 2023

Inkwell

A headless markdown CMS for an independent publisher — collaborative editing, one-click publishing, a decade of archive behind it.

RoleContract engineer TimelineFeb → Nov 2023 TeamMe + the editorial lead StatusDelivered, still running

01 · The problemA literary journal stuck in a copy-paste loop

The publisher ran a quarterly journal and a weekly essay site on a stack of Google Docs, a shared drive named FINAL_v3, and a developer who manually pasted HTML between them. One piece took a full day from accepted draft to live page, and version conflicts were resolved by whoever shouted first.

The brief was blunt: get draft-to-live under an hour, let three editors work on one piece simultaneously, and never lose a revision — without hiring a dev team to babysit it.

02 · How it worksCRDT editor, static output

  • Editing: a Yjs document per piece gives simultaneous edits, inline comments and cursor presence without ever locking a draft; every keystroke lands in append-only history, so "restore last Tuesday" is a single query.
  • Publishing: markdown compiles through a typed remark pipeline into incremental static regeneration — only affected pages rebuild, so a publish touches ~20 files, not 4,000. A forced full rebuild still finishes in under 40 seconds on Cloudflare Pages.
  • Search: Typesense indexes a decade of archive with typo tolerance and relevance tuned for literary queries (author, theme, issue number).
  • Ops: everything on AWS free-tier-adjacent infrastructure — S3 for assets, Lambda for webhooks — because the client could not carry a DevOps bill.

03 · Hardest problemWriters are the harshest QA team

Editors rejected the first two prototypes — not for bugs, but because they broke how prose *feels*: autosave flicker mid-sentence, markdown symbols visible while typing, comment threads that interrupted flow. The turning point was rendering everything as clean typographic HTML (a real serif, real small caps) and treating state changes as invisible. After that, adoption wasn't pushed — the editors started training each other.

"I forgot I was using software." — managing editor, six weeks in

04 · Outcome & lessonsHandover that actually stuck

  • Draft-to-live dropped from a day to roughly ten minutes; the weekly site now ships two extra pieces per month purely from saved production time.
  • Zero support tickets in the final three months of the contract — the runbook and Loom walkthroughs did their job.
  • Lesson learned: for content tools, typography is UX. Features came second to making the page look like the printed journal.
